# Color

Source: Robin Williams, *The Non-Designer's Design Book*, 4th edition, Chapter 7, "Design with Color." Color is a design decision with a structure, the same way type is. Pick a relationship first, then a dominant hue, then lighter and darker versions of it.

## Choose a relationship

Work from the color wheel. Let one hue dominate so the others can accent it.

- **Complementary.** Opposites on the wheel. High energy. Use the opposite as an accent, not as a second background.
- **Analogous.** Neighbors on the wheel. Quiet and related. Good when the piece should feel continuous.
- **Triad.** Three hues spaced evenly around the wheel. Lively. One of the three still has to dominate.
- **Split complement.** One hue plus the two neighbors of its opposite. Contrast with less head-on clash than a straight pair of complements.
- **Monochromatic.** One hue in several tints and shades. Calm, and still capable of contrast if the light and dark steps are far apart.

A tint is the hue mixed toward white. A shade is the hue mixed toward black. Build a small palette of those steps and reuse it. Reuse is repetition. See [repetition.md](repetition.md).

## Watch the tones

Hue is not enough. If every color has a similar lightness, the page goes flat and type can disappear into the field. Separate important items by lightness as well as by hue. Williams' "Watch the tones" and the Wildflower Theory of Color, both in Chapter 7, show how many hues can share a page when their light and dark values still differ. Use the chapter's illustrations before you invent a multi-hue palette.

Warm hues (reds, oranges, yellows) come forward. Cool hues (blues, greens, violets) sit back. A small warm accent can balance a larger cool field. Put the warm color on the element that should be seen first.

Text needs a clear lightness difference from its background. A hue change with almost no lightness change fails this test. On screen, also meet the contrast ratio required by the accessibility standard you are following. See [goodwill-and-access.md](goodwill-and-access.md).

## Print and screen

Print color is built from cyan, magenta, yellow, and black (CMYK). Screen color is built from red, green, and blue (RGB). A color chosen in one model shifts in the other. Design in the model of the medium you will ship. Chapter 7, "CMYK vs. RGB: print vs. web" and "Print vs. web color models," covers the practical difference. Use that chapter when a brand color must survive both a printed piece and a screen.

## Examples

**Do.** A dashboard in a cool gray-blue field, with one warm accent reserved for the primary button and the current step. Success and warning states use a lightness difference, not color alone, so a red-green confusion still leaves the label and the weight.

**Avoid.** Four saturated hues at the same lightness, each used for a different card, with gray text on a tinted card of similar value. Nothing dominates, and the text sinks. A second avoid: a print file exported with unconverted screen colors and no check against a printed proof.

## Review questions

- Which relationship is this palette (complement, analogous, triad, split complement, monochromatic)?
- Which hue dominates, and which hues are accents?
- Do the important elements differ in lightness, or only in hue?
- Does body text separate from its background if hue is ignored?
- Are you choosing colors in the model of the medium you will ship?
