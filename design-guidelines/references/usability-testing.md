# Usability testing

Source: Steve Krug, *Don't Make Me Think, Revisited*, 3rd edition, Chapter 8, "The Farmer and the Cowman Should Be Friends," Chapter 9, "Usability testing on 10 cents a day," and Chapter 13, "Guide for the perplexed." Arguments about what people like do not tell you whether people can use the thing. Watching them try does.

## Religious debates

Chapter 8: teams rehash the same usability questions because each person has strong likes, and each role (design, engineering, marketing) feels those likes as professional truth. People then project their taste onto "most users."

There is no average user. Web use is idiosyncratic. Research that tries to find out what most users like, in the abstract, feeds that myth. The useful question is whether people can finish the tasks that matter, which you learn by watching individuals.

Krug calls the taste arguments religious debates: strongly held, not provable, and rarely persuasive. The antidote is a test. A test often shows that the argument was about the drapes, while the room had no windows: the team was debating a menu style while visitors did not understand what the product was.

## What a usability test is

A focus group is a group talking about feelings, opinions, and past experience. It is useful early, for whether the idea and the value proposition make sense. It does not tell you whether the design works.

A usability test watches one person at a time try to do typical tasks on a site, a prototype, or a sketch. You note where they get stuck. You are testing the design, so call the people participants, not subjects.

Testing one participant is better than testing none. Testing one participant early is better than testing many near the end, because late changes are harder than they look and some users will resist any change.

## A round you will actually do

Krug's do-it-yourself shape, so that testing happens often enough:

- About one morning a month, on a fixed day. In a faster cadence, the same idea is a few users on a regular schedule. The schedule matters more than the milestone, because milestones slip and take testing with them.
- Three participants in a round. Three will not prove anything and will not find every problem. This kind of test is qualitative. Its purpose is to improve the design. You will find more problems than you can fix. Fix the most serious ones before the next round.
- Recruit loosely. Frequent testing beats a perfect demographic match. Almost anyone who can use the kind of product you are testing will hit the serious problems. Tighten recruiting when the task truly requires a specific background.
- Watch together if you can. The same day, the team compares notes and commits to what it will fix. A one- or two-page note of those decisions replaces a long report.
- Test other people's comparable products first if you have never run a session. You learn the method, and you see that confusion is normal.

The out-of-pocket cost can stay very small. If you can hire a professional, do. If you cannot, do it yourself.

## What the agent does

When a review reaches a disagreement about whether people can use something:

1. Write the task in the participant's words ("Find out what this product costs and start a trial"), not the team's feature name.
2. Say what would count as success, and what observation would change the design.
3. Recommend a round of about three participants on the real screen, prototype, or sketch.
4. After the sessions, list the most serious problems, in the order the team should fix them, and leave the rest for a later round.
5. Do not average preferences ("two of three liked the blue header") into a redesign. Krug tells teams to ignore color comments in a test unless the reaction is extreme and repeated. See Chapter 11.

If the blocker is permission, budget, or an organization that treats usability as a specialty name rather than a practice, the tactics for making it happen are Chapter 13. That chapter covers return-on-investment arguments and getting started where you are. Use it instead of inventing a persuasion campaign here.

## Examples

**Do.** A team cannot agree whether filters belong in a sidebar or a top bar. They watch three people try to narrow a list of invoices to "overdue." Two never open the control, because it is labeled "Refine view." The fix is the label and the placement of that one control, checked again next round. The sidebar-versus-top-bar debate waits.

**Avoid.** A late focus group asked which of two visual themes feels more premium, used as evidence that the checkout works. A second avoid: a single forty-page report of eighty problems, with no commitment to fix the three that stopped the task.

## Review questions

- Are we deciding whether people can do a task, or whether we like a design?
- Is the proposed study a test (watch someone try) or a focus group (listen to opinions)?
- What task, in the participant's words, will we watch?
- Which problems will we fix before the next round, and which will we leave?
- Are we early enough that the findings can change the design?
