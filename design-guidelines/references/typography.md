# Typography

Source: Robin Williams, *The Non-Designer's Design Book*, 4th edition, Chapters 9–12 ("The Essentials of Typography," "Type (& Life)," "Categories of Type," and "Type Contrasts"). Type is the design, not a layer added later. Use these rules for headings, body copy, and labels. On a product UI, a platform system font is often the right convention (see [conventions-and-choices.md](conventions-and-choices.md)). Williams' caution about default faces such as Arial and Helvetica is about printed newsletters that look undesigned; it lives in Chapter 8, "Not Helvetica/Arial!".

## Relationships between typefaces

Williams sorts combinations into three relationships (Chapter 10):

- **Concord.** The faces agree. One family, or faces with the same structure, feel calm and formal. Hierarchy then comes from size and weight inside that family.
- **Conflict.** The faces are similar but not the same. Two close sans serifs, or two oldstyle faces that are not a pair, look like a mistake. Avoid conflict.
- **Contrast.** The faces are clearly different, usually from different categories, and they also differ in size, weight, or both. Contrast is the lively, readable pairing.

## Categories

Know which category you are using (Chapter 11). The chapter's quizzes train the eye on serifs and on thick-to-thin stroke contrast; use them when you are unsure what you are looking at.

| Category | What to notice | Where it fits |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Oldstyle | Angled stress, moderate thick-thin contrast, bracketed serifs | Long reading |
| Modern | Vertical stress, hairline serifs, sharp thick-thin contrast | Short display lines; tiring as long text |
| Slab serif | Thick, block-like serifs, little thick-thin contrast | Headlines and sturdy text |
| Sans serif | No serifs; often many weights | UI, headings, and text faces built for body copy |
| Script | Looks written or calligraphic | A few words, never a paragraph |
| Decorative | A strong costume | A title or a logo, never body copy |

## Type contrasts

Chapter 12 lists six ways type can differ. A real contrast uses a large jump on at least one of them, and usually combines two. A small jump on only one is conflict.

- **Size.** A title and the body should not be a size step apart.
- **Weight.** Pair a real bold with a real regular. Neighboring weights look undecided.
- **Structure.** Serif against sans is the clearest structural contrast.
- **Form.** Roman against italic, or capitals against lowercase. Italic is a true drawn italic when the family has one, not a slanted roman.
- **Direction.** A vertical or diagonal line against horizontal text. Use it rarely; it is hard to read.
- **Color.** A real difference of hue or of lightness, including black against a tint. See [color.md](color.md).

All capitals are a form contrast for a few words. A paragraph in capitals is harder to read because words lose their shapes. On screen, reserve underlining for links. In print, underlining cuts through descenders; use weight or italic instead.

## Text mechanics

Chapter 9 is the craft list. Apply it whenever the medium allows:

- One space after a sentence.
- Typographic quotation marks and apostrophes, not straight typewriter marks.
- A hyphen inside a compound word, an en dash in a range, an em dash for a break in a sentence.
- Kern display type when a pair collides or gaps. Leave body text alone unless a pair is visibly broken.
- Keep a heading with the text it introduces. Avoid a single leftover line at the top or bottom of a column (widow and orphan).
- After a heading, start the first paragraph flush. Indent later paragraphs or add space between paragraphs. Do both and the spacing argues with itself.

## Examples

**Do.** A marketing page set in one sans family: a bold 40-pixel title, a regular 18-pixel deck, and 16-pixel body with a line height that lets the lines breathe. A pull quote uses a contrasting oldstyle italic at a clearly larger size.

**Avoid.** A title in one geometric sans and body copy in a slightly different geometric sans, both regular, two pixels apart in size. The pair conflicts. A second avoid: a script face used for a terms paragraph.

## Review questions

- Is this one family (concord) or two categories that clearly differ (contrast)?
- If two faces are both on the page, can you name the contrast (size, weight, structure, form, direction, color) that separates them?
- Is any long text set in capitals, script, or a decorative face?
- Are quotes, dashes, and sentence spacing correct for the medium?
- Does the first paragraph after a heading follow one spacing method?
