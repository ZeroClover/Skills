# Omit needless words

Source: Steve Krug, *Don't Make Me Think, Revisited*, 3rd edition, Chapter 5, "Omit needless words." Krug's third law is a deliberate overstatement he wants followed: cut the words on the page by half, then cut what remains with the same severity. The second cut is there to force a ruthless edit. The first cut is a realistic one. He is applying Strunk and White's rule about needless words to interface copy.

This does not mean shortening articles, stories, or product descriptions that people came to read. It means cutting the words that take up space around the task.

## Why the cut helps

Extra words raise the noise, hide the useful content, and make the page look longer and more demanding than it is. Their presence implies that the visitor might need to read them. Most will not. See [scanning-and-satisficing.md](scanning-and-satisficing.md).

## Happy talk

Happy talk is introductory text that welcomes the visitor, praises the organization, or announces what the section contains, without adding information. Krug's test: if a small inner voice says "blah" while you read it, it is happy talk. It collects on section fronts that are otherwise a list of links. A table of contents does not need a paragraph about how interesting the chapters are.

Good promotional copy stays. It says what is different and what the visitor gets. Happy talk says that you are great. Delete the second kind. A tagline is not happy talk when it answers what this is. See [navigation.md](navigation.md).

## Instructions

People do not read instructions until muddling through has already failed. Wordy instructions fail again at that point. Make the screen self-explanatory so the instructions can go away. See [self-evident-pages.md](self-evident-pages.md).

When a line truly has to stay, cut it to the minimum and put it where it is used. Tell people how long a task takes if that fact helps them decide to start. Move consequences (what happens if they leave a phone number) to the moment those consequences apply. If they must not use this form for a certain request, say so and give the path that does handle it.

Do not teach the names of widgets. People who already know what a radio button is do not need the lesson, and people who do not know the term cannot use the lesson.

## Examples

**Do.** Above a survey: "Help us improve the site. This takes 2–3 minutes." Near the end, beside the contact fields: "Leave a number only if we may contact you about this survey." A separate link, "Contact support," sits with the note that this form does not get a personal reply.

**Avoid.** A 100-word introduction that explains why surveys matter, instructs the visitor to use the drop-down menus and radio buttons, warns about a future phone call before any question has been asked, and mentions customer service without a link. The same facts that are worth keeping are about one sentence each, in the place they are needed.

## Review questions

- If you delete half the words, what task information disappears? Put that back, and cut again.
- Which sentences praise you or preview a list the visitor can already see?
- Which instructions exist only because the control is unclear? Fix the control.
- Is each remaining sentence next to the moment it applies?
- Are any words teaching the interface's vocabulary instead of the task?

## Go deeper
