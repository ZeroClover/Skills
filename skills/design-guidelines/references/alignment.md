# Alignment

Source: Robin Williams, *The Non-Designer's Design Book*, 4th edition, Chapter 3, "Alignment." Nothing is placed arbitrarily. Every element has a visual connection with another element, even when they sit far apart. The purpose is to unify and organize the page. A strong alignment is often what makes a layout look deliberate.

## Rules

Pick one dominant alignment and keep it. Flush left (or flush right, for a right-to-left layout) gives the eye a hard edge to follow. Find an existing edge or axis before you place a new element: the left of the headline, the left of the body, the edge of a photo, the baseline of a neighboring line.

Use centered alignment when the piece should feel formal, still, or ceremonial, and build the center so the centering looks chosen. Centered lines of very different lengths, with no other shared axis, look undecided.

Keep one text alignment on a page. A centered headline over flush-left body, with a flush-right date, asks the eye to start over at every block. If you mix alignments, the mix has to be the idea, and the shared edges still have to be visible.

Align to the content, not to the decorative edge of a box, unless that edge is the axis you intend. On a screen, align labels, fields, and buttons to the same column. In a paragraph, use either a first-line indent or space between paragraphs. Williams treats using both as a spacing error (Chapter 9).

You may break an alignment after the rule is visible and the break is the point, for example a pull quote that steps off the column. A casual break looks like a mistake.

## Examples

**Do.** A settings screen where the section title, field labels, inputs, and Save button share one left edge. Helper text under each field shares that edge too. The column is the connection.

**Avoid.** A title centered, a paragraph flush left, a button centered under the paragraph, and a footnote flush right. Nothing shares an edge, so the screen looks assembled from separate pieces. A second avoid: every block centered, including a six-item list, because centering was the default.

## Review questions

- What does this element line up with?
- Is there one dominant alignment?
- Does centered type serve the tone, or is it the default?
- Do labels, fields, and actions share a column?
- If an element breaks the alignment, is the break obvious and intentional?
