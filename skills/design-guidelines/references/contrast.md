# Contrast

Source: Robin Williams, *The Non-Designer's Design Book*, 4th edition, Chapter 5, "Contrast." If two elements are not the same, make them very different. Contrast does two jobs at once: it makes the page interesting enough to read, and it shows how the information is organized. The eye goes to the contrast. The strongest contrast should be the element you want seen first.

## Rules

Add contrast with typeface, size, weight, line thickness, shape, color, or space. A large heading against a small body, a heavy rule against a hairline, or a bright accent against a quiet field all count.

Make the difference obvious. A sort-of-bold line next to a slightly bolder line, brown text against black text, or 16-pixel type against 18-pixel type reads as an accident. Williams' test: if the items are not exactly the same, make them different. A timid difference is conflict, not contrast. See [typography.md](typography.md) for the same rule applied to typefaces.

Let contrast organize. The reader should see the structure before reading: title, then section, then body, then action. If a decorative element is the loudest thing on the page, it has become the focus, whether you meant that or not.

Combine contrast with the other three principles. Proximity decides what clusters. Alignment connects them. Repetition makes the secondary items match. Contrast separates the primary item from that system. Repeating the contrast (the same strong heading treatment on every section) is what keeps the drama consistent. See [repetition.md](repetition.md).

Check lightness, not only hue. Text that is a close value to its background fails even when the hues differ. For screen text, also meet the contrast ratio your accessibility requirement names. Krug treats weak text contrast as an access failure; see [goodwill-and-access.md](goodwill-and-access.md).

## Examples

**Do.** A report cover with a 56-pixel black title, a 16-pixel gray subtitle, and a small colored rule. The title is unmistakably first. Section headings inside use the same face as the title at a still-large size, so the hierarchy repeats.

**Avoid.** A title at 20 pixels and a subtitle at 18, both medium weight, both dark gray. The reader cannot tell which line is the title. A second avoid: two sans-serif faces that differ only slightly, used as if they were a deliberate pair.

## Review questions

- What should the viewer see first, and is that the strongest contrast?
- Where two elements differ, is the difference large enough to look deliberate?
- Does any contrast create a focus you did not intend?
- Can the reader name the structure (title, section, body, action) without reading sentences?
- Does body text separate cleanly from its background in lightness?
